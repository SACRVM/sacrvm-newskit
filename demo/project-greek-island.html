Project Greek Island

For thirty years, the United States Congress kept a standby capitol buried under a luxury resort in the West Virginia mountains — hidden by a hotel wing, serviced by a television-repair company, and ended not by a spy but by a newspaper article.

A hotel wing with a basement

The cover story was poured in concrete before anyone had to tell it: the bunker went in as an addition to the hotel.

The Greenbrier is an old, grand resort in White Sulphur Springs, West Virginia — springs, golf, presidents among the guests. At the end of the 1950s it also became the answer to a Cold War question: if Washington is destroyed, where does Congress reconvene?

Congress built itself a fallback capitol

Documented record · declassified

Under the Eisenhower administration, ground broke in December 1958 on what the public knew as the resort's new West Virginia Wing1. Beneath the wing, workers poured a government relocation facility built 720 feet into the hillside, finished in October 19622 — a standby home for the entire legislative branch, close enough to Washington to reach, far enough to survive it.

Why the cover heldThe construction was its own alibi: a resort visibly building a hotel wing has cranes, concrete and contractors. Nobody had to explain the digging — the digging explained itself.

The specs of a buried parliament

Facility record · Atomic Heritage Foundation

The bunker measures 112,544 square feet1. Eighteen dormitories held some 1,100 bunk beds — one for every member of Congress and their support staff1. The House would sit in the 440-seat Governor's Hall, the Senate in the 130-seat Mountaineer Room, joint sessions in the Exhibit Hall1. A 400-seat cafeteria had fake scenic windows; Mosler blast doors of up to 28 tons were balanced to open with fifty pounds of force1.

Hidden in plain sightThe chambers were not sealed rooms waiting in the dark. Governor's Hall and the Exhibit Hall worked their whole secret life as ordinary conference space — thousands of convention guests met, dined and watched presentations inside the standby Congress without knowing it.

The television men

A fortress needs maintenance, and maintenance needs people — so the people got a company, and the company got real work.

A TV-repair firm ran the fortress

Documented record · declassified

Day to day, the bunker was kept alive by Forsythe Associates, an Arlington-based contractor ostensibly hired to service the resort's televisions1. Its twelve to fifteen on-site employees were government technicians; roughly a fifth of their time went into genuinely repairing hotel TVs2, which is what made the cover durable — it was not a lie so much as a very incomplete truth.

How much did the town know?

Open question · oral history

The unresolved partThe resort was the region's biggest employer, and locals poured the concrete, wired the rooms, washed the linens. Decades later, residents described wondering about the site to a husband, a wife, a brother — and to no one else1. How many people effectively knew, and simply chose not to know out loud, is not something the record can settle.

smithsonianmag.com · 2024

Undone by ordinary reporting

No defector, no leak of documents — records, local knowledge, interviews, and one Sunday magazine cover.

One article closed a thirty-year secret

Press record · 31 May 1992

On 31 May 1992, The Washington Post Magazine published Ted Gup's "The Ultimate Congressional Hideaway", assembling the bunker's existence from public traces and human sources1. Secrecy had been the facility's real armor — once named, it was useless as a refuge. The government began decommissioning and ended its lease with the resort in 19952.

Never used, now a tour

The bunker outlived its purpose without ever serving it — and became the resort's strangest attraction.

Thirty years on standby, zero activations

Documented record · current

Through the Cuban missile crisis and every alert that followed, the facility was never activated1. Today the declassified bunker is a 90-minute guided tour under the same West Virginia Wing, run by the resort that kept it2 — the blast doors open for visitors on the same fifty-pound hinges.
